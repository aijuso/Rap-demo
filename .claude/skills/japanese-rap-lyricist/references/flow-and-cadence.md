# Flow and cadence for Japanese rap

Use this reference after choosing a writing mode. It treats a lyric as timed
speech: morae, accent, entrances, density, silence, breath, and section-level
energy. It does not prescribe one "correct" flow.

## Working model

Keep four layers separate during diagnosis:

1. **Grid** — beat, subdivision, bar, and phrase length.
2. **Language** — mora sequence, word boundaries, Japanese pitch-accent
   tendencies, particles, and syntax.
3. **Delivery** — onset, duration, stress-like emphasis, pitch movement,
   articulation, breath, and voice quality.
4. **Sound links** — rhyme, assonance, consonance, repetition, and callbacks.

Rhyme is only one rhythmic resource. Mummy-D describes rhyme as one tool for
making rhythm and discusses Japanese accent and pickup placement:
<https://book.asahi.com/article/11630860>

## Morae, not spelling

Japanese timing is often usefully counted in morae, not written characters or
English-style syllables. Treat the following as timing units during a first
pass:

- a simple kana such as `か`;
- a contracted combination such as `きゃ`;
- the moraic nasal `ん`;
- the first half of a geminate consonant `っ`;
- the second mora of a long vowel.

Actual performance may compress, stretch, elide, or anticipate these units.
Therefore, a mora count is a diagnostic, not a metronomic command.

For rhyme analysis, normalize sound rather than kanji. Compare:

- vowel sequence;
- consonant onset where relevant;
- mora count;
- long-vowel and nasal behavior;
- performance pronunciation;
- location in the bar.

Kawahara's phonetic work on Japanese rap provides an empirical basis for
analyzing rhyme through sound structure:
<https://user.keio.ac.jp/~kawahara/pdf/rap2017.pdf>

Kawahara also discusses accent-related observations in rap:
<https://note.com/keiophonetics/n/n3b35320609c9>

## Map the pocket

Before writing full words, annotate four bars:

```text
bar:
entry: downbeat | pickup | delayed
subdivision: eighth | sixteenth | triplet | mixed
anchor_hits:
rests:
phrase_end:
breath:
density: low | medium | high
```

Create at least two candidate pockets:

- a stable version with repeated onset positions;
- a contrast version that changes entry, subdivision, or phrase length.

Choose by audibility and relationship to the beat, not by visual complexity.

## Entry and landing

### Downbeat entry

Useful for clarity, declaration, and a reset after dense passages. Repeating it
without variation can sound square.

### Pickup entry

Begin before the strong beat and land the important word on it. This can make
ordinary Japanese syntax feel propulsive without distorting pronunciation.

### Delayed entry

Leave initial space, then enter after the expected point. Useful for weight,
conversation, and contrast.

### Cross-bar syntax

Let a phrase continue across the bar line when the sentence benefits. The bar
end need not equal the grammatical end or rhyme point. Confirm that the
listener can still recover the syntax.

## Subdivision palette

Use subdivision as an expressive choice:

- **Eighth-note grid:** clarity and space.
- **Sixteenth-note grid:** detail and forward pressure.
- **Triplet grid:** rolling or elastic motion.
- **Sextuplet feel:** finer triplet-derived density.
- **Double-time perception:** more events over the same underlying pulse.
- **Mixed subdivision:** contrast, provided the transition is intentional and
  performable.

The Rittor Music lessons by Machii Def demonstrate beat, offbeat, triplets,
sextuplets, and double-time as practical rap-rhythm concepts:

- <https://plugplus.rittor-music.co.jp/training/series/rap_lesson/lesson01/>
- <https://plugplus.rittor-music.co.jp/training/series/rap_lesson/lesson02/>

Do not label a passage "double time" merely because it contains many characters.
Judge audible event rate against the pulse.

## Density contour

Write a section-level contour instead of maximizing density:

```text
bars 1-4: medium, establish pocket
bars 5-8: sparse, foreground image or claim
bars 9-12: dense, escalate
bars 13-15: mixed, destabilize
bar 16: space plus decisive landing
```

The exact contour must follow the beat. 田我流 describes avoiding
overcrowding, building gradually, and making subtle pace changes with track
developments:
<https://www.redbull.com/jp-ja/rasen-15-dengaryu>

R-指定 describes a planned fine-grained flow turning into a larger groove
through interaction with the beat. Preserve such productive accidents if they
sound stronger:
<https://www.billboard-japan.com/special/detail/4723>

## Japanese syntax and naturalness

Particles and auxiliary forms make Japanese flexible, but flexibility is not
permission for arbitrary inversion. Audit:

- Does the particle attach unambiguously?
- Does the predicate arrive before working memory loses the subject?
- Is an omitted subject recoverable?
- Is the register consistent?
- Is a phrase only present to complete a rhyme?
- Would a native speaker say this outside the rhyme?

Small words can be placed lightly before or after a strong point, but do not
swallow information-bearing particles so completely that the relation changes.

## Accent and emphasis

Japanese pitch accent is not identical to English lexical stress. Rap delivery
can alter pitch, duration, intensity, and timing, but an override should sound
intentional.

Use this order:

1. Speak the phrase naturally.
2. Identify the semantically important mora or word.
3. Place the phrase so the important point aligns with a useful beat location.
4. Modify pitch, duration, or intensity only as needed.
5. Record and check whether the word remains identifiable.

Do not assume that placing every lexical item on a strong beat creates
emphasis. Contrast creates emphasis.

## Rhyme placement within flow

Distribute sound links across positions:

- line-end rhyme;
- internal rhyme;
- chain rhyme continuing across bars;
- delayed resolution after one or more intervening phrases;
- multisyllabic or multi-mora correspondence;
- assonance or consonance when exact rhyme would force language;
- repeated syntactic frame with changing terminal sound;
- callback to a sound introduced in the hook.

Rhyme density should have a contour. A plain landing after a dense chain can be
more forceful than another rhyme. R-指定 discusses intentional non-rhyme:
<https://miyearnzzlabo.com/archives/66634>

For each rhyme, verify three independent dimensions:

```yaml
sound: audible similarity in the performed pronunciation
meaning: both phrases make independent semantic contributions
placement: the listener can perceive the relation in time
```

If only `sound` passes, the rhyme is not ready.

## Phrase break and enjambment

An unexpected break can create suspense or reframe the next word. Use it only
when:

- the partial phrase creates a recoverable expectation;
- the continuation supplies a payoff;
- the pause is audible;
- breath permits the intended gap;
- the device is not repeated mechanically.

AKLO discusses unexpected phrase breaks and a final-line reveal:
<https://music.fanplus.co.jp/special/20160607660229aeb>

## Breath, articulation, and stamina

Create a breath map for every complete verse:

```text
/  planned full breath
,  micro-breath
~  sustained sound
x  risky consonant cluster or tongue transition
!  semantic word that must remain clear
```

Hard requirements:

- No planned full breath may split a word.
- A breath after a particle must not make its relation ambiguous.
- Two high-density bars require either a viable breath or a deliberate
  performance technique the writer can execute.
- The final word of a long phrase must retain enough air for articulation.
- Live-performance feasibility must be assessed separately from a comped studio
  take.

MFS describes planning flow variation and ad-libs for a long verse while
treating physical stamina as part of the performance:
<https://www.redbull.com/jp-ja/rasen-06-mfs>

## Ad-libs

Ad-libs are arrangement elements, not decoration. Assign each one a job:

- reinforce a key word;
- answer the lead voice;
- mark a section transition;
- fill a deliberate gap;
- alter character or emotional temperature.

Reject ad-libs that mask an unclear lead, imitate a named artist's signature
tag, or occupy every available gap.

## Performance revision protocol

Record three takes:

1. **Natural speech take:** preserve ordinary pronunciation.
2. **Pocket take:** prioritize rhythmic relation to the beat.
3. **Clarity take:** exaggerate consonants, phrase ends, and referents.

Compare them without looking at the page. Build the final from the best
principles, not by mechanically comping every word.

Tag audible failures:

| Tag | Failure | Typical repair |
|---|---|---|
| `LATE` | Intended landing drifts after the beat | Remove a mora, enter earlier, or change the preceding pause |
| `RUSH` | Phrase accelerates unintentionally | Reduce syllabic load or choose a finer stable subdivision |
| `MUSH` | Consonants or particles disappear | Open space, change word order, or simplify adjacent sounds |
| `AIR` | Breath collapses the ending | Move breath, shorten phrase, or lower sustained demand |
| `MONO` | Every bar has the same contour | Change entry, density, pitch, or phrase length |
| `GRID` | Correct timing but no groove | Test pickup, delay, swing-like microtiming, or larger phrase grouping |
| `FORCED` | Pronunciation exists only to serve rhyme | Replace rhyme or recast the full line |
| `LOST` | Syntax/referent cannot be followed by ear | Restore particle, repeat referent, or simplify the clause |

Re-record the repaired line with its neighbors. VIGORMAN's account supports
iterative recording and adjacent-line revision:
<https://www.redbull.com/jp-ja/64bars-vigorman>

Mummy-D argues for performing and recording rather than composing only through
intellect:
<https://fika.cinra.net/article/201910-mummyd_kngsh>

## Minimum deliverables for a lyric

When the task includes performance design, return:

1. BPM and assumed meter, or a note that no beat was supplied.
2. Section and bar labels.
3. Entry/density contour.
4. Breath marks.
5. Optional stress/landing annotation for difficult passages.
6. A clean lyric without annotations.
7. Any line whose feasibility remains uncertain.

Never claim exact beat fit without hearing or inspecting the beat.
